# How to enter published data
You who are about to enter published data, we salute you! Thank you for making these data open and accessible to the community! (Remember, published data should always be assigned to the Public group.) Recently published papers should be straightforward and relatively easy to enter, but entering data that predates GPS units, morphotype systems, and descriptive schemas, to give three examples, can pose challenges. Below, we provide best practices for entering published data, based upon our own experiences. The information given below is ordered based on the order of PBot entry.

## At least one specimen is required to create an OTU. How do I choose the best exemplar (preferably a type) specimen(s) to enter?
To some extent, the answer to this question depends on what information is available for each specimen listed as type material and/or figured in a publication. However, we recognize that the information needed to create a Specimen node (and the accompanying Collection node) may be difficult to impossible to find in papers published a long time ago. 
*	Ideally, you should enter the primary exemplar specimen used in the published description. The order of preference is holotype or lectotype or neotype > syntype > paratype > hypotype. You should choose a figured specimen.
*	If there are other figured specimens that you would like to feature in the images of the OTU, you must enter those specimens in addition to the primary exemplar. This will allow you to add those images later.
*	If the OTU has already been entered in the Plant Fossil Names Registry (PFNR) or the International Fossil Plant Names Index (IFPNI), then much of the information you need may be easily found there. If not, consider entering formally named taxa there, as well as in PBot!
*	Remember, you need to find and enter the locality data (i.e. Collection node) for the specimen before you can actually enter the specimen.

## What if there is poor locality information? (i.e., it’s difficult to create the Collection node)
Do your best to georeference the site, recognizing that this can be really difficult for fossils collected a long time ago, and use the “GPS coordinate uncertainty” field to account for the uncertainty in the location. For example, if all you know is that the specimen is from the U.S. state of Wyoming, use Google Maps or another resource to determine the latitude and longitude of the approximate midpoint of Wyoming. Then set the GPS coordinate uncertainty to the approximate radius of Wyoming (300,000 meters).

Sometimes you can find the record(s) for the type specimen(s) in the repository’s publicly accessible collections database, and we have found cases where the locality information, including gps coordinates and uncertainty, is more complete in the museum’s records than in the original publication. It is a good practice to look for those online records to cross reference information for the Collection node, and for adding a link to the repository’s record in the Specimen node. Make sure to indicate in the notes fields what information came from specimen metadata rather than directly from the original publication. 

If the paper does not include any lithological data about the site, choose “Sedimentary” for the lithology field.

## What if the taxonomy is outdated?
Prior to the use of morphotypes, paleobotanists commonly assigned fossil specimens to modern genera, without the data to back it up. This is particularly problematic for otherwise outstanding monographs published in the late 1800’s and early 1900’s. (For the North American dicot workers, we provide the examples of Knowlton, Lesquereux, Berry, Brown, etc). Here, we list best practices for creating OTU nodes from historic publications.
* If the generic assignment is questionable (e.g., the many OTUs that were assigned to the modern genus Ficus in the early paleobotanical literature), place the genus name in quotes (ex: “Ficus” obovata). Ideally, there should be a published reference supporting the use of quotation marks and this reference should be entered into PBot and listed as a reference supporting the OTU. If the generic assignment has not yet be directly questioned in the literature, but you feel strongly that this questionable assignment should be noted, we suggest the following: (1) provide a written explanation in the notes field, (2) use quotation marks around the genus only in cases where a living genus was used and likely inappropriate. PBot is not intended to override what is validly published, yet we also want to prevent confusion by workers that may be more unfamiliar with the history of paleobotanical naming practices and evolving systematics that are trying to use fossil data to inform other analyses (for example, in phylogenetic analyses).
* What do I enter for the taxonomic information? For most cases, in the PBDB parent taxon field, you will choose the higher rank parent taxon that is explicitly stated in the published taxonomic treatment (even if you believe it is incorrect and needs revision), and then enter the published taxonomic information (family, genus, specific epithet) into the appropriate fields in the Optional fields accordion menu. You may use quotes in the free text fields to indicate clearly questionable assignments as mentioned above. In the Notes field, please include any taxonomic opinions from the published description or subsequent works. You may also record your comments on the taxonomy here to aid future users or workers. Hopefully, recording such opinions and dialog will help prompt the needed formal revisions. We strongly suggest to check the Plant Fossil Names Registry (PFNR) and the International Fossil Plant Names Index (IFPNI) for the nomenclatural status of the scientific names you are entering. These two resources are actively building authoritative registries of fossil plant names based on published literature and contain helpful information on nomenclatural acts, typification, and references, etc. 

## How do I enter a published description?
There are three key aspects of the description node architecture that are important to understand in order to make best use of the PBot’s capabilities for handling different scenarios. 
1.	Descriptions are applied to one or more specimens, not directly to a taxon. This is because specimens are the primary data that the description is based upon, and associating descriptions with the specimens themselves allows for specimen-level variation in traits to be recorded and employed in useful ways. It is also practical; for example, if a specimen is re-assigned to a different OTU, the specimen’s description transfers with it. 
2.	A specimen, or group of specimens, can have multiple description nodes. This allows users the flexibility to add multiple interpretations of specimens by different authors, as well as apply multiple different types of descriptions using different schemas to specimens (as explained further below). 
3.	Description nodes are schema-specific; therefore, you may create more than one description node to describe features of the fossil specimens that are circumscribed by separate schemas (for example, a leaf’s morphology from the Manual of Leaf Architecture schema and the functional physiognomic traits from that leaf using the Dicot Quantitative schema).
Note that these three aspects in combination can account for a wide range of case scenarios. For example, a group of specimens could have a shared description of leaf morphology based on the Manual of Leaf Architecture schema. One specimen in this group has cuticle preserved and therefore has an additional description using the Cuticle Schema, while a different subset of specimens have been measured for functional traits and recorded via a the Dicot Quantitative schema.

In practice, you should create separate description nodes for each schema that you plan to use and for each reference or person who described the specimen(s). Make sure the relevant specimens and references are entered before attempting to create a description. 

First, create a description node for a specific schema, named following the conventions in the node naming guidelines above. This description node will contain the relevant part of the description from the publication, with as little interpretation as possible.
*	Choose the relevant schema for this description node (e.g., MLA for dicot leaves), select the published reference, and select the specimens for which this description applies. We encourage you to list all Specimens that you were able to enter from the publication in the entry form for this Description. 
*	Copy the publication’s description (or the diagnosis if only a diagnosis is given) verbatim and put this in the “Written description” optional field. Only copy the relevant part of the description for the schema chosen to describe the specimen(s) (for example the leaf part of the description for a leaf specimen described by the MLA). 
*	After creating the description node, go back and edit that description node to add the character instances for characters that were explicitly stated in the publication, minimizing interpretation as much as possible. To stay faithful to the reference that you are entering, this will sometimes mean that you cannot score every character that you can see and interpret on the specimens (whether via the specimen figures or on the actual specimens). 
*	If you would like, you can create an additional description node in which you describe, based on your own observations or interpretations, one or more specimens featured in the publication. This is a great way to update descriptions that might not use modern terminology or conventions while separately maintaining the integrity of the original author’s interpretations. Name this node “OTUName SchemaName by YourLastName” (ex: Davidia antiqua MLA by Currano). You may choose to assign this description to a private group, particularly if you are working on a taxonomic revision, but remember to make the description public upon publication.

Repeat this process for parts of the published description that are described by other schemas.

## Diagnoses vs. Descriptions
According to the International Code of Nomenclature for Algae, Fungi, and Plants, “A diagnosis of a taxon is a statement of that which in the opinion of its author distinguishes the taxon from other taxa.” A description is defined as “a published statement of a feature or features of an individual taxon… a validating description need not be diagnostic.” Ideally, publications will have separate sections labeled “diagnosis” and “description.” If this is the case, then the publication’s diagnosis section should be copied verbatim into the diagnosis field in the OTU entry form, and the relevant text from the publication’s description section should be copied verbatim into the “written description” optional field in the Description entry form (see below). If the publication only has a description section or only has a diagnosis section, then the text there should be copied verbatim into both the diagnosis field in the OTU entry form and the “written description” optional field in the Description entry form.

## Node Name Cheat Sheet
PBot requires all node names to be unique. Below, we provide regulations and guidelines for naming the different node types.
* **Collection Name:** Choose a unique identification that may aid in recognizing close links among Collections. For example, if you are entering a series of consecutive beds, you could provide a location name and meter level (e.g., “Saddle Mountain 1.5 meter bed” and “Saddle Mountain 2.0 meter bed”). Names must be under 80 characters for compliance with the PBDB.
* **Specimen Number:** When entering the “Specimen number,” write the repository code before the repository’s catalog number if it is not already part of the catalog number. For example, a specimen at the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History should begin with USNM followed by the catalog number, resulting in a unique Specimen number “USNM PAL777113”. Do not forget to include the repository code, as different institutions may use the same numbering system and Specimen numbers must be unique in PBot. If a catalog number has not been assigned, a field number or other tracking may be temporarily used and edited at a later time.
* **Description Name:** For published OTUs/Specimens, we recommend the format “OTUName/SpecimenNumber + Author(s) + SchemaName + Published Description” (ex: Davidia antiqua Manchester MLA Published Description). For unpublished work or new descriptions of published OTUs/Specimens, we recommend the format “OTUName/SpecimenNumber + SchemaName + by AuthorLastName” (ex: Davidia antiqua MLA by Currano). Other acceptable and informative names include: UCMP 24678 preliminary cuticle description or DE 124 morphotype aggregated MLA. Note that this informative name for a description will be useful when specimens are later re-described or more completely described by another worker. In such a case, the original description (whether simply a text description or scored schema characters) can be retained and a new description node created for the revised descriptions. Both descriptions can be viewed and the historical information is retained alongside newer interpretations.
* **OTU Name:**
  * For OTUs that are informally named and include a systematic affinity to a formal name (e.g., Lauraceae sp., Aralia sp., cf. Rhus), the OTU name should be systematic affinity + morphotype code (if applicable) + locality/flora (e.g., Lauraceae sp. WW036 Bighorn Basin, cf. Rhus Bighorn Basin).
  * For OTUs that are informally named with no systematic affinity (e.g., Morphotype 27), the OTU name should be the locality/flora + morphotype code or name (e.g., Cerrejón Morphotype CJ38, Mush Long & Loopy, Bighorn Basin Dicot sp. FU735 Hairy Leaf).
  * For formally named, published OTUs, the name should simply be the full published name (i.e., the binomial consisting of genus + specific epithet). **Importantly, only use the Linnaean binomial name if you are entering the official taxonomic record (whether original or revised), complete with the published reference, diagnosis, and at least one type specimen.** If you are creating an OTU record because you are entering material that is identified as a known taxon that is not yet in the database, then we encourage you to find the original description and type/exemplar specimen and create the public official OTU record for that species. Then you may simply assign the additional new specimens to that taxon. If you are not able to enter the original record for a published/named OTU, as an interim solution you can add a descriptor for your locality to the end of the OTU name (e.g., Platanites raynoldsii Big Bluff) to differentiate it from the official OTU record. You may edit/add to this record if you are later able to find and enter the actual diagnosis, reference, locality and type specimen for the official OTU, at which point you can edit the OTU name to essentially convert it to the official record for the taxon. Alternately, if you discover that someone else has entered the official OTU, then you can reassign your specimens to that OTU node; any Descriptions you have made will carry over, as they are tied to Specimens, not OTUs. Remember to delete your old OTU after reassigning the specimens.

## What if there is no appropriate schema in PBot to describe my fossils? 
If there is not an appropriate schema available that applies to the organ/preservation that you are describing, then you may do one of the following. (1) select the “General text description only schema” and enter the description in the “Written description” field. No characters or states exist in this schema. (2) Create a custom schema for describing your fossils. Note that your schema will need to be made “public” in order for users to see your description. Anyone may use a public schema, and in this way PBot may be able to facilitate building schemas for description of different aspects of fossil plants.

## How can I include images/illustrations of specimens?
First, check to see if the specimen’s repository has an online image of specimens. If so, enter the stable URL for that image. If not, take a screenshot from the publication and upload that to the PBot Internet Archive collection or upload directly to PBot. And if you should happen to visit the specimen, please take a great photograph and create a new Image node. Note that PBot has a limit on the size of images uploaded. If you encounter this problem, you must downisize the image or host the image on a different site instead and link to it from PBot. Make sure you have permission to upload these images or that any images that you are linking or uploading are public domain! Many online collection databases and journals hold copyright over their specimen photographs.
